# How do I mix a watercolour that matches the colour I see?

You can see the colour: the grey-green of a slope in shadow, the warm stone of a wall at dusk. Turning it into a puddle on the palette is harder than it looks, and watercolour makes it harder than any other medium.

There is no white to lighten a colour; water does that, and water changes the value as much as the hue. The paint dries lighter and duller than it looks wet. Every extra pigment takes a share of the light away, so a mix that is corrected three times turns heavy. And once a wash is on the paper, you can add to it but hardly take anything away. So the work happens before the brush touches the sheet: at the palette, and on a scrap of paper beside it.

## Three decisions in every mix

### Which paints

Find the hue first, then the two paints in your box that lie on either side of it. A dull olive sits between a yellow and a blue, but nearer the yellow and pulled down by something warm. A violet-grey sits between a blue and a red-brown. If one of your paints is already close, start with that one alone and look at what is missing.

Two paints reach nearly every colour in a landscape or a street: greens, oranges, violets, browns and greys. A third paint is for the colours two cannot reach, usually a muted or dark colour where the third shifts the hue. More than three is where mud begins.

### In what ratio

A ratio needs a unit. The simplest is the **brush load**: the same brush, loaded the same way, every time. Three loads of yellow to one of blue is a ratio you can repeat tomorrow; "a bit more blue" is not.

Start with the lighter or weaker paint and add the stronger one in small amounts. A phthalo blue or a dioxazine violet can take over a mix in a single load, and you cannot take it out again.

### How much water

Water sets the value: how light or dark the wash will dry. It helps to think of a few fixed steps rather than an endless scale: from a whisper of tinted water, through a light and a medium wash, to a rich one and to paint so concentrated it barely flows. The chapter [Washes](washes.html) describes five such levels, with the proportion of paint to water each one roughly needs and what it looks like in the well.

Mix the paint for the strongest wash you need first, then take part of the puddle and thin it for the lighter ones. One puddle at several strengths keeps the colours of a picture related, and saves you from matching the same colour twice.

## Matching one particular colour

When the colour is in front of you, in a photo or in the subject, the work is a short loop:

1. **Name it.** Hue, temperature, value: "a cool, middle-dark blue-grey" is already half a recipe.
2. **Choose two paints** from your box, and decide which is the larger share.
3. **Mix it strong**, in a small puddle, where the ratio is easy to judge. Then add water for the value.
4. **Test it** on a strip of the same paper, and let it dry completely.
5. **Compare** the dry stroke with the colour, in the same light, and adjust one thing at a time.

Adjusting has a few simple rules. Too pale: add paint in the same proportion, not more of one paint. Too warm or too cool: add a little of the other paint in the mix. Too bright: a touch of its complement. Too dull: you cannot make a mix brighter again; mix it once more with fewer paints. [Mixing in practice](mixing.html#test-before-you-paint) goes through the test strip in detail.

## Keeping mixes clean

- **Look at the pigments.** A paint's label lists its pigments (PB29, PY150 and so on). Two paints with three pigments each are already six in the puddle.
- **Build darks from transparent paints.** Opaque pigments in a dark mix make it chalky.
- **Mix greys from complements**, not from black: ultramarine with burnt sienna gives every grey from blue to brown as you shift the ratio.
- **Accept the limit of a paint.** A paint cannot get darker than its own colour at full strength. When a colour is darker than any paint you own, it needs a second, darker paint in the mix, not more of the first.

## What a calculated recipe cannot know

A recipe is calculated, not painted. The app knows each paint from one reference colour on white paper. It does not know your batch of paint or a pan that has dried for years, how opaque a paint is, or whether it granulates. It also cannot see your paper. So the recipe is a starting point that is usually close; **the test strip on the same paper decides**. When your strips keep disagreeing with a paint, [calibrate it](calibrating.html) from your own swatch, and the recipes follow your paint instead of a printed chart.

The photo is a reference for colours and values, not a pattern to copy. You decide what to keep, what to leave out and how loose to paint it.

## Questions and answers

### How many paints should a watercolour mix have?

Two paints reach nearly every colour; a third helps with muted or dark colours that two cannot reach. Beyond three, mixes tend to turn dull, because every pigment takes away part of the light.

### How do I make a watercolour lighter without white?

With water. The same mix at more water dries lighter, while the ratio of the paints stays the same. Mix the strongest wash first, then thin part of the puddle for the lighter areas.

### Why does my mix look different when it dries?

Wet paint looks darker and richer than dry paint, because water fills the paper's surface and reflects less stray light. Judge every mix dry, on a strip of the same paper, next to the colour you want.
