# How do I plan watercolour glazes, layer by layer?

A glaze is a thin wash of one transparent paint, laid over a layer that is completely dry. The light passes through every layer, bounces off the white paper and comes back through them all. That is why glazed colour glows: a green made of a yellow layer and a blue layer over it is clearer than the same green stirred in a well, because each film of paint stays a single, transparent pigment.

The technique itself is quickly learnt. What takes practice is the plan: which paint goes first, where each layer goes, and when to stop. A glaze can only darken. Every layer you add takes light away, and lifting it again in a glazed painting damages what lies below. So the decisions come before the brush, and they come in a fixed order.

## Which paints glaze well

Three properties of a paint decide whether it belongs in a glaze, and most makers print them on their charts.

- **Transparency.** Transparent paints glaze cleanly; semi-transparent ones work in thin layers. Opaque paints, the cadmiums, Naples yellow, cerulean and many earths, lay a veil over what lies below. Keep them for the top layer or leave them out.
- **Staining.** A staining paint (phthalo blue, the quinacridones, Prussian blue) bites into the paper and hardly moves once dry. That makes it a good first layer: the next glaze cannot lift it.
- **Granulation.** Ultramarine, the cobalts and many earths settle into the grain of the paper, and that texture shows through every layer over it. Fine in a stony foreground, distracting in a clear sky.

A simple glazing palette is three or four paints: a transparent yellow, a transparent blue, a red or rose, and an earth such as burnt sienna for the darks and to quieten colour. With these, the overlaps give greens, oranges, violets and deep, living darks.

## The order of the layers

### Light to dark

Plan the light first. Decide what stays white paper, what gets only the first pale layer, and work towards the darks. The first layer usually covers the most, often nearly the whole sheet; the last ones touch only small areas.

### One paint per layer

Each layer is one paint at one strength. The colour of an area comes from which layers it receives. A hillside gets yellow and blue and turns green; the sky beside it gets only the blue. If an area needs to go darker in one paint, it gets a second coat of the same wash, but a second coat of a paint never goes darker than that paint at full strength. Darker needs another paint on top.

### Warm and cool

A cool glaze over a warm passage pushes it back; a warm glaze over a cool one brings it forward. A thin blue over a distant ridge makes it recede; a touch of warm earth over a foreground rock brings it closer. Know what each layer is for before you load the brush.

### How many layers

As many as the picture needs, and no more. Three to five layers carry most paintings. Each one costs drying time and a small risk of disturbing what lies below. If a layer brings only a slight shift, leave it out; a fresh passage is worth more than a perfect value.

## Planning glazes from a photo

When you paint from a reference photo, the plan is a map: for each area, which layers it receives and how many coats. Doing this by eye for a whole landscape is slow, and the usual mistakes are a layer painted where it was not needed and a dark that two layers would have reached on their own.

1. **Find the paints.** Look at the main colours of the photo and choose the three or four paints whose overlaps reach them.
2. **Map the first layer.** Mark everything that receives it, which is everything except the lightest lights and the areas that need none of that paint.
3. **Map each further layer** on its own, in order from light to dark.
4. **Test the overlaps** on a strip of the same paper: each paint alone, each pair, and the darkest combination. Let the strip dry.
5. **Paint one layer, let it dry completely**, then the next. Not touch-dry: dry, the paper flat again and no longer cool.

## What a plan cannot know

The app treats every dried wash as a transparent filter. Real layers are not quite that: an opaque paint covers more than the plan expects, a granulating one leaves texture, and a layer painted before the one below was dry lifts it and turns dull. So the plan is a sound starting point, and the test strip on your paper decides. ## Questions and answers

### What is glazing in watercolour?

Glazing is painting a thin, transparent wash of one paint over a layer that is completely dry. The colours mix optically on the paper instead of in the palette, so the result stays clear and luminous.

### How long should a layer dry before the next glaze?

Until it is completely dry, not just touch-dry: the paper is flat again and no longer feels cool against the back of your hand. A layer that is still damp inside lifts under the next glaze and turns muddy.

### Which watercolour paints are best for glazing?

Transparent paints, and staining ones for the first layers, because the next glaze cannot lift them. Keep opaque paints such as the cadmiums for the top layer, and use granulating paints where their texture is welcome.

### How many glazes can I paint over each other?

Three to five layers carry most paintings. Each layer darkens and takes some of the paper's light; after five, colour tends to lose its glow.

### Is glazing better than mixing?

Neither is better. Glazing gives clearer, more luminous colour and suits large, calm areas. Mixing in the palette suits many small, distinct colours and areas you want to finish in one pass. Many paintings use both.
