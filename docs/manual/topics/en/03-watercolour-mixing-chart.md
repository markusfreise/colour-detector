# How do I make a mixing chart of my own watercolours?

A maker's colour chart shows each paint alone. It does not show what your ultramarine does with your burnt sienna, or whether your lemon yellow and your phthalo blue give a green you would ever use. A mixing chart does: a grid with your paints along the top and down the side, and in every square the mix of the two.

It is the most useful reference a watercolourist can paint, and it only works if it is yours. Paint it with your paints, on the paper you paint on. The same pair looks different on another paper, from another maker, or from a pan that has dried for years.

## Before you start

### Choose the paints

Take the paints you actually use, not the whole range you could buy. Six to twelve paints is a good size: six gives 15 pairs, twelve gives 66. More than that and the chart becomes a project of its own; split it into two charts, warm and cool, or one per palette.

Order them like a colour wheel: yellows, oranges, reds, violets, blues, greens, then the earths and greys. Neighbours on the chart are then neighbours in colour, and the pattern of the chart is easier to read.

### Draw the grid

On a sheet of your painting paper, draw a grid in pencil with one row and one column per paint. Squares of two to three centimetres are large enough to judge and small enough to fit twelve paints on a half sheet. Write the paint's number and name along the top and down the side in pencil; ink can bleed when the paper gets wet.

The squares along the diagonal, where a paint meets itself, are for the paint alone. The two halves of the grid repeat each other, so you only need one of them. Use the other half for the same pairs at a different ratio, or leave it empty.

## Painting the chart

1. **Paint the diagonal first.** Each paint alone, at a medium strength: clearly coloured, still flowing easily.
2. **Mix each pair in equal parts.** Count them in brush loads, one load of each, and stir them together in a clean well before you add water. A ratio is easier to judge in a small, strong puddle.
3. **Paint the square in two strengths.** The upper half strong, the lower half with more water. Many pairs change character when they are thinned: a dull mix at full strength can be a beautiful pale grey.
4. **Clean the brush and the well** between pairs. A trace of the last mix in the next one is enough to spoil it.
5. **Let the chart dry completely** before you judge it. Watercolour dries lighter and duller than it looks wet.

If you want more from a pair, paint a short row instead of a single square: three loads to one, equal parts, one to three. That row shows how the mix travels from one paint to the other, and it is the row you will use most when you match a colour.

## Reading the chart

A finished chart is a map of your box. Look at it in daylight, a step back from the table.

- **Clean pairs.** Paints close on the colour wheel make bright secondaries: a lemon yellow with a phthalo blue, a rose with an ultramarine. These are your greens, oranges and violets.
- **Neutral pairs.** Paints opposite each other on the wheel cancel into greys and browns: ultramarine with burnt sienna, a red with a green. The best of them give living neutrals; the weak ones go flat. Mark the ones you like.
- **Muddy pairs.** Some squares look dull at every strength, usually where two paints of several pigments each meet. Note them and avoid that pair.
- **Strong paints.** A paint that takes over every square it touches, a phthalo, a dioxazine violet, needs far less than equal parts. Write that beside its name.
- **Gaps.** Look for colours in your pictures that appear in no square: a clean orange, a cool violet, a deep green. That is where a new paint would earn its place in the box.

## Questions and answers

### What is a watercolour mixing chart?

A grid with your paints along the top and down the side, and in each square the mix of the two paints. It shows what every pair of your paints gives on your paper, which a maker's chart of single paints cannot.

### How many paints should a mixing chart have?

Six to twelve. Six paints give 15 pairs, twelve give 66. Take the paints you actually use; a chart of a whole range takes days and is hard to read.

### Should I mix equal parts for the chart?

Equal parts in brush loads is the simplest start. For pairs you use often, paint a short row instead: three to one, equal parts, one to three. Strong paints such as the phthalos need far less than equal parts.

### Why paint each square at two strengths?

Because many pairs change when they are thinned. A mix that looks heavy at full strength can be a clear, beautiful tint with more water, and the chart should show both.

### Does Watercolourlove replace a mixing chart?

No. The app works out recipes from your paints for every colour of a picture, but it knows each paint from one reference colour. Your chart shows how the paints really behave on your paper; use it to check the app, and calibrate a paint when the two disagree.
