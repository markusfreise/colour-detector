# Which colours of a reference photo do I actually need to mix?

Zoom into any photo and every patch of grass, every stone, every shadow turns out to be dozens of slightly different colours. A camera records all of them with the same care. A painter cannot mix them all, and should not try: a watercolour with a hundred puddles is slow to paint and rarely looks better than one with twenty.

So the first step from photo to painting is not mixing at all. It is deciding which colours carry the picture, which ones can share a puddle, and which ones the camera got wrong.

## Look past the detail

### Squint

Half close your eyes. The detail blurs, small variations merge, and what remains is a few large shapes, each with one colour. Those are the colours the picture is built on. A hillside that is a thousand greens up close becomes a light green, a middle green and a dark one. A sky becomes one blue that pales towards the horizon.

### Separate hue from value

Two colours can differ in two ways. They can be different **hues**, a green and a brown, or the same hue at different **values**, a light green and a dark green. In watercolour the difference matters a great deal, because value is set by water. A pale green and a deep green of the same hue are one puddle at two strengths, not two mixes.

So when you list the colours of a photo, group them by hue first. Each group is one mix. Then note how many steps of light and dark each mix needs. That list is far shorter than it first looks.

### Keep the small accents

Grouping has one danger: a small area of a distinct colour gets swallowed by its large neighbour. The red roof in a green valley, a yellow boat on grey water, the lips in a portrait. These cover little of the picture but carry a lot of it. Give them a mix of their own, even if it is only used once.

## Where the camera misleads you

A photo is a reference, not the truth. Three things are worth correcting before you mix.

- **Shadows go dark and grey.** A camera exposes for the whole picture, and what is darker than it can handle turns flat and nearly black. Your eye, in front of the same place, sees colour in the shadow. Paint it a little lighter than the photo and give it the colour of what it falls on: a shadow on grass has green in it, a shadow on stone the colour of the stone.
- **Bright skies burn out.** Pure white in a photo is often a sky the camera lost. The paper can stay white, but a pale wash may bring back what was there.
- **Colours look dull.** Phone photos especially flatten colour. The violet in a shadow, the warmth in a stone are there, only weaker. Painters exaggerate them anyway.

## How many colours to aim for

The right number depends on the picture and on how much time you want to spend at the palette.

| Picture | Mixes |
|---|---|
| A quick study, a bold poster-like picture | 6 to 10 |
| Most landscapes and streets | 12 to 20 |
| A careful portrait or a picture full of small colours | 20 to 24 |

Each mix is a puddle you prepare, test and keep wet. Above about two dozen, the gain is usually smaller than the extra work.

## Simplify the shapes too

Choosing colours and choosing shapes go together. A photo with few colours but countless tiny shapes is still exhausting to paint. Before you mix, decide which shapes you would paint as one stroke and which you would let dissolve into their neighbours. [Values and simplifying](values-and-simplifying.html) goes into that decision in depth.

The app measures the photo; it does not know what the picture is about. It keeps the colours the photo has, including a shadow the camera turned too dark or a sky it burnt out. The photo is a reference for colours and values, not a pattern to copy.

## Questions and answers

### How many colours do I need for a watercolour from a photo?

For most landscapes and street scenes, 12 to 20 mixes are enough. Each mix is painted at several strengths of water, so 20 mixes give far more than 20 colours on the paper.

### How do I find the main colours of a photo?

Squint at it. The detail blurs and a few large shapes remain, each with one colour. Group those colours by hue: each group is one mix, and its lighter and darker versions are the same mix with more or less water.

### Should I copy the shadows of a photo exactly?

No. A camera makes shadows darker and greyer than they were. Paint them a little lighter and give them the colour of what they fall on.
