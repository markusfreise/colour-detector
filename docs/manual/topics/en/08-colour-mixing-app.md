# Can a colour mixing app tell me how to mix a watercolour?

Yes, within limits worth knowing. Mixing a colour is partly craft and partly arithmetic. The craft is at the desk: loading the brush, judging the puddle, laying the wash. The arithmetic is the question before it: of the paints in my box, which two or three come closest to this colour, in what ratio, and how much water does it need? That second part is what a good app can take off your hands, for one colour or for every colour of a picture.

## What an app can work out

Watercolour behaves in a way that can be calculated. The paint lies on white paper as a thin, transparent layer; light passes through it, bounces off the paper and passes through it again, and every pigment takes away part of the light. That is subtractive mixing, the same law that describes coloured glass or ink in water. Given a good reference colour for each of your paints, an app can:

- **Try every combination.** Each paint alone, every pair, every group of three: far more than anyone tests by hand, in a moment.
- **Find the ratio** that comes closest, and say how close it comes.
- **Set the water.** Since water, not white, makes a watercolour lighter, a pale and a deep version of one colour are the same mix at different strengths. A good app tells you which level, not only which paints.
- **Respect the limits of paint.** A paint cannot get darker than its own colour at full strength. When a colour is darker than any paint you own, it needs a second, darker paint, and the recipe should say so instead of asking for more of the first.

## What it cannot know

An app knows each paint from one reference colour. It does not know:

- **your tube or pan.** Batches differ, and a pan that has dried for years is not the chart.
- **opacity, staining and granulation.** An opaque paint covers more than a transparent model expects; a granulating one adds texture the screen shows as a flat colour.
- **your paper and water.** A cream paper warms every colour; a paper that soaks up quickly changes how a wash settles.
- **what the picture is about.** It can measure a colour. It cannot decide that a shadow should be warmer than the photo, or that a detail should go.

So a calculated recipe is a starting point that is usually close. The test strip on the same paper decides.

## What to look for in a mixing app

- **Your own paints.** A recipe from paints you do not have is little use. Look for real paints by maker and number, with their pigments, and a way to enter any paint yourself.
- **Calibration.** You should be able to replace a chart colour with your own swatch, so the recipes follow your paint.
- **Water levels.** A recipe for watercolour needs a strength, not only a ratio.
- **A rating.** An honest app tells you when your paints cannot reach a colour, instead of offering a poor match as a good one.
- **Privacy.** Reference photos are often personal. It is worth knowing whether a photo is uploaded, and where it goes.

## Where it is honest about its limits

The app knows each paint from one reference colour and models every paint as transparent; it does not model granulation or staining. Dark paints such as Payne's grey or sepia start out lighter than they really are at full strength, because the stock colours come from a chart that shows a mid-strength wash. When your strips keep disagreeing, [calibrate the paint](calibrating.html) from your own swatch. The app proposes; the colours, the shapes and the brush stay yours.

## Questions and answers

### How accurate is a colour mixing app for watercolour?

As accurate as its idea of your paints. With good reference colours the recipes are usually close, but an app cannot see your paper, your batch of paint or how opaque it is. Test each mix on a strip of the same paper.
